# Andrea's Editing Guide

- **Top priority is accuracy.** If something doesn't feel right, check it out! Verify names, titles, dates, numbers against source material whenever possible. Confirm spelling of unfamiliar names and places.

- **Pull your reader in immediately with a poignant quote or vivid description.** Front load the most important, engaging information. The lead line and first paragraph must be crystal clear.

- **Declutter!** Eliminate any words that do not advance the story. Often, less is more. Do not overcrowd the first paragraph with too much information or context. That can come in subsequent paragraphs.

- **Avoid acronyms!** If they must be used, spell out what they mean in the first reference. Avoid jargon like legal lingo as well.

- **Read the story out loud.** If you run out of breath during a lengthy sentence, consider breaking the narrative into two shorter, more powerful lines.

- **If something doesn't make sense to YOU, it won't make sense to your audience.** Clarify or consider deleting it, if you don't lose context or an important element.

- **Don't reference someone's age or clothing, unless it is relevant.** (i.e., If a business owner is "just 17 years old," or a mechanic is wearing "a grease-stained shirt.") Age may be relevant in many, but not all, crime stories.

- **Aim to use only compelling quotes!** Limit officials giving statistics, and with official and expert quotes, be specific with attribution (A police official from which location? An expert from what industry? Provide names and titles.) Leverage overheard dialogue which can be just as relevant.

- **Be mindful that it's your responsibility to transport the audience to the scene.** Support reporters' efforts to include at least two of the five senses in their narrative: sight, hearing, smell, touch, taste.

- **Headlines and photo captions:** Confirm the headlines match the actual content and the captions match the photos.
